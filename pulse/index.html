Why this holds up

01

Systematic querying, documented method

A fixed query set — the questions a clinician, a purchaser, and a patient would actually ask — run across the major assistants, repeated to capture variance rather than screenshotting one lucky answer.

02

Findings traced to the sources behind them

Every notable answer is mapped back to the public material the model appears to be drawing on: your own site, registries and clearance databases, society guidance, review sites, forums, and competitor content.

03

An action plan, not just a scorecard

Where an answer is wrong or absent, the report names the source layer that would have to change, ordered by how much of the answer it accounts for and how much control you have over it.

Questions we get

Which AI assistants are covered?

The major general-purpose assistants clinicians actually reach for, plus the AI answer surfaces now embedded in search. The exact list is stated in the report’s method section and dated, because coverage changes as products launch and change.

How often do these answers change?

Often enough that a single screenshot proves nothing. That is why the method repeats each query across sessions and phrasings and reports the variance, and why the deliverable is explicitly a dated snapshot rather than a standing score. Re-running the same query set later is how you measure whether anything you changed worked.

Is this just SEO?

Related, not identical. Classic SEO optimises for a ranked list of links; this optimises for what a model says when no list is shown. The overlap is that both are driven by public source material — the difference is which sources carry weight, how they are synthesised, and the fact that there is no ranking to inspect. The report deals in what the answers say and where they come from.

What does the deliverable look like?

A scorecard with a row per query and a column per assistant, a source map, a findings list, and an action plan — plus an appendix of the dated raw responses. Roughly the shape of a test report, which is deliberate: it is meant to be re-run and compared, not admired once.

Can we use the findings in marketing?

Carefully, and that is your regulatory call, not ours. The report is informational: it documents what assistants said on a date. Turning any of it into a promotional claim — particularly a comparative one — is subject to your promotional review and the applicable advertising and labelling rules, and the report says so on its face.

What if an assistant says something factually wrong about our device?

The report separates that case from framing problems and from absence, because the fix is different. Factual errors usually trace to a specific public source or to an absence the model filled in; the action plan names the source layer to address and what a correction there would plausibly do. What no one can offer you is a switch that edits the model.
