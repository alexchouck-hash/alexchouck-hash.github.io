How it works

  1. SUR-01

    Confidential first conversation

    A mutual NDA before you describe the concept in detail. One call, typically an hour, to understand the clinical problem and what you have already tried.

  2. SUR-02

    IP landscape scan

    A structured search of granted patents, applications, and published devices adjacent to the concept, with a written summary of what is crowded and what looks open.

  3. SUR-03

    Design and build

    Concept sketches, then CAD, then a machined prototype in a representative material. You see the design before it is cut.

  4. SUR-04

    Handover

    The prototype, the CAD files, the IP scan, and a regulatory pathway memo — plus an honest conversation about whether this is worth continuing.

What the work looks like

The offer

The sprint engagement

Pricing is fixed once we have seen the concept at the scoping review, or a reduced fee plus an agreed equity share if you are building a company around it — your choice, written into the agreement before work starts.

Intake is limited. A sprint ties up a design engineer and a machine, so we run a small number at once, and the review is where we tell you if your concept is not one we should take.

The lead time depends on the concept: a hand-instrument variant and an implantable are not the same job, and quoting them the same way would be dishonest. You get a date and a price in the same document.

What you receive

  • A machined prototype in a representative material, plus the CAD files.
  • An IP landscape scan: what is granted, what is pending, what is published, and where the concept sits relative to it.
  • A regulatory pathway memo: likely classification, likely route, and the evidence that route would demand.
  • A written recommendation, including the case for stopping if that is what the scan supports.

What you own when it is over

  • The concept and any invention arising from it are yours. That is stated in the agreement, not implied.
  • The CAD files and the prototype are delivered to you.
  • Our licence is limited to what we need to do the work you asked for, and its scope is written into the statement of work.
  • The IP scan is a search, not a legal opinion — you and your attorney decide what to do with it.

Questions we get

Who owns the intellectual property?

You do. The concept is yours before the sprint and any invention arising from it is yours after. We take only the limited licence needed to perform the work you engaged us for, and its exact scope is written into the statement of work you sign — not left to a general terms page. If you are working under an institutional agreement, your employer’s IP policy may also apply; we will ask about it early rather than late.

How is my idea protected before I sign anything?

A mutual non-disclosure agreement is signed before the first detailed conversation. You do not have to describe the mechanism to get a meeting.

What does "prototype" actually mean?

A machined part or assembly in a representative material — something with real geometry, real tolerances, and real feel in the hand. It is a feasibility and communication tool, not a clinical device: it is not sterile, not validated, and not for use in or on a patient.

What does a sprint typically cost?

It depends on the concept, and quoting a single number here would be a guess. The fee is fixed once we have seen the concept, and quoted before any work starts. If you would rather trade fee for equity, we will discuss that in writing first.

What if my idea already exists?

That is exactly what the IP scan is for, and finding out in week two for a fixed fee is the cheapest possible version of that news. Frequently the answer is more useful than a straight yes or no: something adjacent exists, and the gap between it and your idea is the actual invention.

I have no company and no funding. Is this still for me?

Yes. Most clinical insight arrives to someone with a day job and no entity. The pathway memo and the prototype are the two artefacts that make the next conversation — with a company, an investor, or your institution’s tech transfer office — a real one.
