Free tool · in design

How long will clearance take? Answer with a distribution, not folklore.

Not live yet. This is a design preview — the interface below is rendered, not recorded from working software, and the figures in it are invented. It is here because the shape of the tool is settled. The weekly brief carries it first when it ships.

Every device company has a number in its head for how long clearance takes. It usually came from a conference conversation in 2019. The actual decision times are in the public record, per product code, and they are not one number — they are a spread.

What it does

You give it a product code. It reads FDA's own 510(k) records for that code over a window you choose, and reports what actually happened: how many clearances, the median time from receipt to decision, and the tail — the 80th percentile, which is the number your schedule should actually be built on.

Then it shows you the exact query it ran. Screenshot the number and the query together into your program review, and the person who asks "where did that come from" has their answer on the same slide.

A median is a comfortable number. The 80th percentile is the one that decides whether your launch date survives.

Why a distribution and not an estimate

Because an estimate is a claim about your submission, and nobody — including us — can make that claim. What review actually takes depends on your device, your predicate, the quality of your file, and how many additional-information rounds you draw. None of that is knowable from a product code.

What is knowable is what happened to everyone else in your code. That is a useful, honest input to a schedule, and it is a different kind of statement from a forecast. The tool will not produce a single number for your device, because that number would be fiction with a decimal point on it.

What it reads

The public openFDA 510(k) endpoint. Decision dates and receipt dates are part of FDA's published clearance records, which is what makes this computable at all — the tool is doing arithmetic on public data, not modelling anything.

Both dates the calculation needs — date_received and decision_date — are present on every one of the 175,879 records in the dataset. We checked, because a percentile computed over a field that is only sometimes populated is a wrong number delivered confidently, which is exactly the failure this tool exists to prevent.

Two records carry a malformed decision year. Any bounded date query excludes them, which is how the tool queries anyway.

One real answer, with its query

Since the whole argument is that a number should travel with its source, here is one, computed from the live API on 28 August 2026 for product code LLZ:

ClearancesMedian80th percentileRange
n = 390141 days246 days7–728 days

The query that produced it:

GET api.fda.gov/device/510k.json
  ?search=product_code:"LLZ"
   +AND+decision_date:[2021-01-01+TO+2026-08-17]
  &limit=1000

Note the gap: the median is 141 days and the 80th percentile is 246. A schedule built on the median is a schedule that is late four times in ten. That gap is the entire reason to look at the distribution instead of repeating a number you heard at a conference.

Dataset last updated 17 August 2026. Re-run the query and you will get a slightly different answer, which is the correct behaviour for a number about the world.

What it will not do

  • It will not predict your clearance date. See above; that is the whole design.
  • It will not tell you why the tail is long. The record has dates, not reasons.
  • It will not take your data. You give it a product code. There is nothing to keep.

Why we are giving it away

Because the number is public and the arithmetic is not hard, and because the way a firm handles an uncertain number tells you most of what you need to know about working with them. We would rather show you that with a free tool than assert it on an about page.

If the tail in your product code is the thing worrying you, that is a conversation about file quality — which is what the gap scan and the DHF buildout are for.
